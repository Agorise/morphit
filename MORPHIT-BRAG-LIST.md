## 1. Free, fast, and friction-free

1. **Signup is free to the user.** No credit card, no bank link, no fee, no deposit. The operator's relay account pre-mints Account Creation Tokens (ACTs) at ~100 BLURT each in a weekly batch ceremony, then consumes one ACT to create your account on-chain — so you join cost-free, and the operator's hot wallet is never sized to the daily signup rate. (See ADR-0010 §4 for the key-custody rationale.)
2. **No KYC, no ID, no selfie, no proof of address, no nothing.** You submit your cryptographic public keys and your desired username. That's the entire signup.
3. **No email required. No phone number or sms. No identity verification.** No "for legal reasons we need..." — none of it. The system literally cannot ask for those things because nowhere in the architecture is there a place to store them.
4. **Listing fee is roughly $0.12 per order.** A trade on a centralized exchange costs more in spread alone.
5. **Send a chat message to a stranger for ~$0.01.** Welcome to a marketplace where contacting a counterparty costs less than a stamp. Spammer/solicitor filters are also built-in.
6. **Three-second block confirmations.** New orders show up in the orderbook in three seconds — fast enough to prevent the eBay-style last-second sniping that plagues some other P2P platforms.
7. **No transaction-fee auctions.** No gas wars. No priority-fee arms races. Listing fees are flat (with a Sybil-tier multiplier for spammers, see §9).
8. **First buy of BLURT is free.** New users get a one-time waiver on their first listing fee, which puts real BLURT in their wallet so they can post and participate from day one.
9. **No deposits.** Nothing to "fund" before trading. You hold your own coins; you trade when you want.
10. **No withdrawal fees.** Because there's nothing to withdraw FROM. Trades settle peer-to-peer between you and the counterparty's wallets, not through an exchange custody account.

## 2. Privacy by design (not by promise)

11. **No cookies. No analytics. No logging.** The footer says it because it's true, not because the lawyers wrote it.
12. **We do not get, store, log, transmit, or otherwise touch your IP address.** Period. Zero. The relay extracts the client IP only as a rate-limit bucket key in memory; it's never written to disk, never logged, never sent anywhere, and the in-memory entry is discarded as soon as the rate-limit window passes. The source code (`apps/relay/src/middleware/ip.ts`) carries this as a binding contract — adding IP logging would require a security advisory and an ADR.
13. **We encourage VPN, Tor, Lokinet, and I2P access.** Anything that hides your IP from the server you're talking to is a good idea. Our anti-abuse defenses are deliberately designed not to punish privacy-conscious users — rate limits are coarse, easy to evade with a VPN rotation, and we accept more abuse rather than make privacy-conscious users feel unwelcome. The Tor `.onion`, I2P b32, and Lokinet endpoints are first-class citizens, not afterthoughts.
14. **No central key store.** Your Blurt private keys never leave your device. There's no key database for anyone — including a future-bankrupt operator — to leak.
15. **No session cookies.** No login state on the server. No session hijacking class of vulnerability because there are no sessions.
16. **No password reset emails.** Because there are no passwords stored. You hold your own keys; the project can't forget what it never knew.
17. **End-to-end encrypted (E2EE) chat.** Buyer-seller conversations are encrypted on your device, then stored on the Blurt chain as ciphertext. Not even the indexer can decrypt them.
18. **End-to-end encrypted (E2EE) chat uses per-message ECIES (X25519 + ChaCha20-Poly1305-IETF, libsodium primitives).** Each outbound message generates a fresh sender ephemeral key that's wiped after one use — that gives you sender-side forward secrecy: even if your posting key leaks later, an attacker cannot decrypt messages YOU sent in the past. We're honest about the tradeoff: the receiver's long-term chat key is stable until you rotate your posting key, so we don't claim per-message receiver-side forward secrecy. Full design + tradeoff rationale in `docs/adr/0015-chat-crypto.md` and the `forward_secrecy` FAQ entry.
19. **Deliberately NO Double Ratchet — and we'll defend the choice.** The Signal-style Double Ratchet protocol gets a lot of (deserved) press for forward secrecy and post-compromise security in messengers, but we evaluated it against Morphit's actual threat model and explicitly rejected it. Two reasons: **(a)** the realistic compromise scenario for a Morphit user is "your Blurt posting key leaks" (lost device, malware, weak passphrase) — and a posting-key leak lets the attacker re-derive every chat key you've ever held, which defeats forward secrecy and post-compromise security regardless of how clever the ratchet is; **(b)** shipping a full ratchet implementation means a ~2 MB WASM crypto bundle, which would more than double Morphit's first-load size and meaningfully hurt access on slow connections (mobile data, Tor, low-end hardware). What we ship instead is a layered defense designed for our actual threats: **per-message ECIES with a fresh sender-ephemeral key wiped after one use** (gives sender-side forward secrecy without the WASM overhead), **chain-anchored TOFU pinning** that detects any attempt to swap out a peer's chat key after first contact, and **opt-in out-of-band 8-word fingerprint comparison** that closes the first-contact MITM window for users who want belt-and-suspenders. Each layer is covered by smoke tests; each tradeoff is documented in `docs/adr/0015-chat-crypto.md`. This is what "secure messaging tuned to a specific threat model" looks like — not "use the most-marketed protocol regardless."
20. **Opt-in out-of-band fingerprint verification ("Verify peer").** For users who want belt-and-suspenders MITM protection beyond the chain-anchored TOFU pin, the conversation menu has a "Verify peer" item that computes an 8-word fingerprint from your chat keys, derived locally on your device with the PGP word list (deliberately NOT BIP39 — we don't want users mistaking it for a seed phrase). Compare with your counterparty via voice call or in person. Hidden by default, no badge, no nag, no telemetry — most users will never see it. Power users get the protection that the rest of secure-messaging research calls "safety numbers" without imposing the friction on everyone.
21. **Private E2EE chat history is permanent and verifiable.** Stored on chain forever, signed, timestamped. The immutability matters for posterity (your trade record can never be deleted by a bankrupt operator), for legal recourse (an unredactable contemporaneous record is courtroom-grade), and for reputation integrity (counterparties can't quietly delete inconvenient threads to manipulate their feedback story).
22. **No Cloudflare.** The project deliberately rejects Cloudflare and similar centralized reverse-proxy services that intercept all user traffic at TLS termination.
23. **No Google Analytics, no Facebook Pixel, no Hotjar, no LogRocket.** No third-party trackers, full stop.
24. **No third-party CDN for fonts or scripts.** Everything self-hosted; your browser doesn't phone home to Google Fonts when you load a page.
25. **The frontend has a strict Content-Security-Policy.** No external scripts, no inline event handlers, no `eval`, no dynamic code paths.
26. **The relay only speaks to pre-configured Blurt RPC endpoints.** No SSRF. No "fetch a URL the user supplies" code paths.
27. **Scans your text for accidental private-key disclosure.** When you type into a chat or feedback box, Morphit scans the text for WIF keys, 64-character hex, and 12/24-word seed phrases. If detected, it warns you in red. If you ignore the warning, it truncates the key client-side before it leaves your device. Keep your private keys private!
28. **Profile fields don't require real names.** Use a handle. Use anything. Profiles are decoration over a cryptographic key "identity". Be as anonymous as you want to be.
29. **Sharing your public address goes through a privacy-aware modal.** Not just a copy-paste field — a flow that asks about subaddress preferences (XMR), and amount-jitter (XMR). Untraceability is the mission.

## 3. Security and audits — receipts, not slogans

30. **Over 1,960 self-checking smoke scenarios** ship with the source code. Run them yourself: `bash scripts/run-smokes.sh`. They cover the indexer, relay, ops CLI, frontend bus, payments, federation probe, fee verification, chat encryption, and more.
31. **A running audit document** (`docs/AUDIT-2026-05.md`) — currently 9,600+ lines across 27 numbered parts, organized by date and subsystem, listing every security review pass, every finding, every severity rating, every fix or accepted-risk rationale. Public, in the repo, anyone can read it. Plus per-batch audit files in `docs/audit/` (Batch I YubiKey unlock, Batch J release trust anchor, Batch K block explorer, Batch L payment methods).
32. **STRIDE threat-model methodology** applied per audit pass: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, Elevation of Privilege — a matrix run over every changed subsystem, with attack trees built from the most attractive entry points.
33. **Adversarial red-team narratives.** The audit doc names hypothetical attackers (a doxxing journalist, a federated phisher, a sanctions evader, a reputation-launderer) and walks through what each would actually do to the system. Defenses are designed against the playbook, not against generic "bad actors."
34. **AUTOMATION-AUDIT.md** — separate document covering the build pipeline, dependency hygiene, and supply-chain attack surface.
35. **REVISIT-LIST.md** — public list of every accepted-risk item or deferred fix, with full context. Nothing falls through the cracks; nothing is hidden in a private bug tracker.
36. **AUDIT-FINDINGS.md** — historical findings catalog, severity-tracked.
37. **PHASE-F-AUDIT.md, PHASE-G-PREP-AUDIT.md** — phase-specific deep-dive audit reports.
38. **Threat-model assumes the attacker has read every line of code.** The codebase is AGPL-3.0; this assumption is realistic, and designing for it is what makes the system actually safe.
39. **Reproducible builds.** Every release tarball can be rebuilt byte-for-byte from its tagged commit. The build script publishes a `verify.json` containing per-file SHA-256 hashes; the same manifest is recorded on the Blurt chain via a `morphit_release_v1` op.
40. **On-chain release attestation.** Every Morphit release's bundle hashes are broadcast to the Blurt blockchain. Your browser can verify that the page you're loading matches the manifest the project published — independent of the operator's word.
41. **Two independent verification paths**: (a) GPG signature on the source tarball, (b) on-chain hash manifest on the running bundle. If either is wrong, escalate; if both match, trust.
42. **SHA-256 + SHA-512 + GPG signatures** on every release, via the `release-sign.sh` script. Operators can verify that their downloaded source matches what the project published.
43. **No PHP, no WordPress, no XML-RPC, no OAuth, no Express middleware sprawl.** Whole vulnerability classes that plague other platforms simply don't apply here.
44. **No `eval`, no `Function()`, no dynamic-code paths anywhere.** The CSP enforces this; the codebase is clean.
45. **Strict CORS allowlists** on every API endpoint. No promiscuous `Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *`.
46. **64 KiB request body cap** on every endpoint. No "send us a 500 MB JSON and watch our server fall over" attacks.
47. **Per-IP rate limiting** at the indexer's middleware layer: 120 req/min for list endpoints, 600 req/min for single-record lookups, both operator-tunable. The IP is used as an in-memory bucket key only — never logged, never persisted.
48. **Schema-validated request bodies** via Zod with bounded field lengths. Malformed input never reaches handler code.
49. **No SQL string concatenation.** All queries are parameterized; SQL injection isn't a thing here.
50. **Constant-time comparisons** for invite tokens and HMAC verification, to defeat timing oracles.
51. **Signed, short-lived (10-minute) invite tokens.** A cryptographic hash of the requesting IP is included inside the signed token to prevent trivial cross-IP replay — but the IP itself is never logged or persisted; only the hash exists, only inside the token, only for the 10-minute TTL. After that the hash is gone with the token.
52. **Altcha proof-of-work challenge** on the Nth invite per user per day. Frictionless for honest users, expensive at scale for spammers.
53. **Global daily ceiling** on signups, configurable per operator. Caps worst-case Sybil drain regardless of how many IPs an attacker controls.
54. **Operator-balance scanner** alerts when the relay's BLURT balance approaches zero, so the operator can refill if desired before signups stall.
55. **Federation probe** auto-discovers other Morphit instances and tracks their health (good / quiet / stale / unreachable / mismatch / never).
56. **YubiKey unlock support** for the local key vault on supported browsers (ADR-0017).
57. **Optional secp256k1 key isolation** via local hardware token; private keys never enter the page's JavaScript heap when this mode is on.
58. **Matrix-only security disclosure channel** at `@agorise:matrix.org` — end-to-end encrypted (E2EE) by default, no email-in-cleartext disclosure path.

## 4. Real decentralization, not the marketing kind

59. **Federated orderbook over a public blockchain.** Orders live on the Blurt chain. Any operator running a Morphit indexer sees the same orderbook in real-time, and a buyer on one instance sees orders posted through any other instance — each operator's frontend is its own brand and URL, but the data layer is shared. Multiple indexers cross-verify each other.
60. **No central server to attack.** Take down a Morphit website and the federation continues; a buyer just opens another operator's URL.
61. **No central database to subpoena.** The orderbook isn't IN a database; it's ON the public Blurt chain. Subpoenas aren't a useful attack here.
62. **No single point of failure.** Operators come and go; the chain is permanent.
63. **Federation directory built into every instance.** Visit `/instances` on any Morphit URL and see a list of every other known instance, their alt-network addresses, and their health status.
64. **Reachable over Tor.** Onion services are first-class; instances commonly publish a `.onion` address alongside their public hostname.
65. **Reachable over I2P.** Instance directory tracks `.b32.i2p` addresses; the federation probe tries them.
66. **Reachable over Lokinet.** Same for `.loki` addresses. Different threat model than Tor; sometimes faster; an option, not the only path.
67. **Reachable over Nostr.** Operators can publish their pubkey as an alt-network channel for users who prefer that ecosystem.
68. **No registrar, no domain auth, no DNS dependency required.** A Morphit instance can run on `morphit.local`, on `.onion` only, on `.loki` only — DNS is a convenience, not a requirement.
69. **Anyone can run a node.** Pick a domain (or skip the domain), clone the repo, run the setup script, broadcast a `morphit_operator_register_v1` op. You're in the federation. No application, no permission, no central listing.
70. **Operators get 90% of BLURT-paid fees on their instance, paid immediately, per-order.** Real revenue model, automated on-chain split, operator-direct payment with no project skim. The relay queues a `transfer` op the same transaction the order op is indexed in; total wall-clock latency from user "Post" click to BLURT landing in the operator's wallet is typically 10-15 seconds. Not "we'll Venmo you next month" — closer to a card-processor's instant settlement, except non-custodial. (BTC/XMR fees go 100% to project treasury for ecosystem support documented in `docs/FEES-AND-REWARDS.md`.)
71. **Operator instances are self-branded.** `acme.example.com` running Morphit looks like Acme Corp's marketplace, not like a generic affiliate page.
72. **Run on a Raspberry Pi.** Hardware requirements: 2 cores, 2 GB RAM, 20 GB SSD, 1 Mbit/s. A Pi 4 with a USB-3 SSD is sufficient for a community instance.
73. **Run on a spare laptop.** Closed-lid on a shelf with wired Ethernet works great. The laptop battery doubles as a UPS.
74. **Run on a $5/month VPS, or $0/month at home on residential internet** with Dynamic DNS. No static IP required; many operators host from home.
75. **No Blurt witness or full chain node required.** The indexer talks to public Blurt RPC endpoints over HTTPS.
76. **Indexer cross-verifies via multiple Blurt RPCs.** No single chain provider is a trust anchor.

## 5. Non-custodial, honestly

77. **Morphit never holds your funds. Period.** The actual buyer-seller trade — fiat going one way, crypto the other — happens directly between the two parties' wallets. Morphit is a bulletin board, not a bank. No deposits to fund, no balances to withdraw.
78. **No escrow account.** No "we hold while the trade settles" pattern. Settlement is direct.
79. **No custody fees.** Because no custody.
80. **No "frozen account" mechanism.** Thanks to the Blurt blockchain, demonetization, censorship and user bans are impossible.
81. **No exchange-side hot wallet to be hacked.** Because no exchange-side wallet.
82. **No insolvency risk.** A bankrupt Morphit operator can't owe you BTC because they never had your BTC.
83. **No FTX failure mode.** No SBF holds the keys. There are no shared keys. There is no shared cold storage.
84. **The trade itself never appears on Morphit's books.** Your bank transfer goes from your bank to the seller's bank. The XMR moves directly from seller's wallet to buyer's wallet. Morphit cannot see, log, or intervene.
85. **You can audit the on-chain receipts.** Every fee paid, every listing posted, every feedback signed — provable on the Blurt blockchain, no Morphit cooperation required.

## 6. For Monero users specifically

86. **Monero is a first-class asset, not an afterthought.** Listed alongside Bitcoin and BLURT as the three core trading assets. Treated as a peer, not a curiosity.
87. **Morphit won't accept your view key.** Ever. For any reason. The Monero verification flow is explicitly designed to NOT require it.
88. **Morphit never proxies your XMR transactions.** They go directly between the two parties' wallets — Morphit doesn't even broadcast on your behalf.
89. **Amount randomization (jitter) defeats correlation attacks.** When you share your XMR address, Morphit can append cryptographic-RNG randomness to the trailing 6 decimals of the amount — up to ~0.000001 XMR (1 microXMR ≈ $0.0002 trivial cost) — so two "0.5 XMR" trades produce distinct on-chain amounts. Defeats the well-known view-key amount-correlation attack.
90. **Jitter uses `crypto.getRandomValues`, not `Math.random`.** Predictable PRNG would let observers correlate jitters across one user's trades. Morphit uses cryptographic-quality randomness.
91. **Jitter is asymmetric — round UP only.** Never underpays the seller. Verifier compares observed amount vs expected; underpayment fails. Costs the buyer at most 0.000001 XMR per trade.
92. **Subaddress nudge.** When you try to share a standard Monero address (starts with `4`), Morphit gently suggests using a subaddress (starts with `8`) instead. Standard addresses link every received payment to the same view key; "Stealth" subaddresses break that linkage. Not paternalistic — it's a soft nudge with a brief explanation.
93. **Multi-explorer attestation for XMR proofs — five independent explorers by default.** When verifying that a Monero payment landed, Morphit queries five Monero block explorers in parallel (xmrchain.net, localmonero.co/blocks, monerohash.com/explorer, exploremonero.com, moneroexplorer.org — all running the same `onion-monero-blockchain-explorer` reference codebase, but operated by independent parties) and rejects when responding explorers disagree on the proven amount. Operator-configurable to any compatible list, including self-hosted instances for maximum independence (see `OPERATIONS.md §40.4`). No single explorer can lie about a verification undetected.
94. **The "I sent the funds" flow includes XMR-specific tooling.** TxID copy-paste, view-key-handling explanations, integrated subaddress hints — Monero-aware throughout, not just "another asset on the dropdown."
95. **Monero-specific FAQ entries** in 10 locales explaining: how to find a TxID in GUI / Cake Wallet / Feather / monero-wallet-cli, why subaddresses matter, why Morphit won't accept your view key, how amount jitter protects you, and what the limits are.
96. **No "show us your wallet balance" prompt.** Ever. Other platforms ask. Morphit doesn't.
97. **No KYC trigger thresholds for Monero trades.** No "trades over $X require ID." No KYC at all, ever, regardless of trade size.
98. **Onion-only access works for Monero traders.** Several operators run `.onion`-only instances; you can trade XMR without your IP ever touching the clear net.
99. **Per-asset payment-method registry.** Operators can list which payment rails they support per asset, with operator-specific notes — reduces wasted DM exchanges asking "do you take Cash App for XMR?"

## 8. Reputation, trust, and chat that survives the platform

104. **Star ratings on chain, signed by both parties, immutable.** Every feedback row is a Blurt `morphit_feedback_v1` op signed by the reviewer. Edit-proof, delete-proof, fake-proof.
105. **Reputation can't be faked.** Your displayed star average is computed only from on-chain feedback rows whose `reviewer` signed the op AND whose feedback is tethered to a real on-chain order (not free-form). Self-signing isn't possible — the chain rejects ops without a valid signature from the reviewer's posting key — and the indexer further excludes (reviewer, subject) pairs flagged in `suspicious_reciprocity` (sock-puppet pattern) or `related_accounts` (linked-account heuristics). What's left, averaged and rounded to 2 decimals, is what the world sees.
106. **Positive feedback builds your reputation.** Every counterparty who rates you 4 or 5 stars after a real on-chain trade lifts your average. Keep in mind: if you want people to trade with you, your reputation is everything. New accounts show up with an `is_new_trader` flag (fewer than 4 ratings) — and counterparties may be wary of trading with someone whose reputation has yet to be established.
107. **Sock-puppet detection.** The indexer runs a `suspicious_reciprocity` heuristic that flags pairs who exclusively review each other. Suppressed feedback is excluded from the headline rating but visible in the raw history.
108. **Verified-chat badges.** Feedback rows get a "verified chat" badge if the reviewer-subject pair had a real-looking on-chain conversation BEFORE the review: at least 2 messages from each side (bidirectional, not one-shot), at least 15 minutes between first and last message, and the pair isn't flagged in suspicious-reciprocity. Defeats the "fake-trade-fake-review" pattern by requiring evidence of actual interaction.
109. **Feedback responses are themselves on-chain.** When you reply to feedback you received, the response is signed and attached. No "edit out the bad review" pattern.
110. **Engagement counter** shows how many distinct accounts messaged the order owner about a specific order in the last 24 hours. Tells you if an order is alive or stale.
111. **Loyalty milestones** delegate progressively more BP (10/50/200/1000, totaling 1,260 BP) as you accumulate cumulative BLURT-fee spend. Real reward for sustained good-faith trading.
112. **Welcome bonus on first completed trade: 10 BLURT liquid + 10 BLURT Power (BP).** BP is your own vested, staked BLURT — not a delegation, not borrowed. You own it. Staking earns you curation rewards, empowers your upvotes, and earns you ~2% interest (APR).
113. **First-fee welcome BP** delegates 1 BP on your first BLURT-paid listing fee, separate from the welcome bonus. Small symbolic stake giving you a foot in the broader Blurt ecosystem.
114. **Reputation can't be migrated to a competitor's silo.** It's on a public chain. Your reputation is yours, portable across every Morphit instance. If you want to start using a different operator's frontend, your reputation comes with you.

## 9. Anti-spam and anti-Sybil (without surveillance)

115. **Listing fees rise with abuse.** Sybil-tier multiplier scales: 4th order in 24h = 1×, 5th = 2×, 6th = 4×, 7th+ = 8×. Honest traders pay $0.12; spammers pay rapidly-growing tolls.
116. **Cold-message fees** discourage drive-by spam. First-time DM to someone you've never traded with costs ~$0.01 in BLURT, escalating with abuse history.
117. **Featured-slot bidding is auctioned.** Top-of-orderbook placement requires outbidding — but minimum-hours floors prevent micro-bid sniping, and bids are paid to the operator (not to a project skim).
118. **Account creation costs the operator 100 BLURT per signup.** That's a real economic gate. Sybil farms attacking a Morphit instance must convince the operator's relay to spend real money on each puppet account, which the operator's daily-ceiling defenses cap.
119. **Per-IP signup spacing.** Multiple invite-token issuance from the same IP triggers an Altcha PoW challenge.
120. **`/v1/health` short-circuits signups when the relay is low on funds.** Drains stop before they become unbounded.
121. **Drainer has defense-in-depth.** Per-row caps (N BLURT max amount per queued transfer), savepoint isolation per op, idempotency guards.
122. **No invite is "verified by SMS / phone."** No carrier surveillance. The invite system uses cryptographic proof-of-work, not telecom data.
123. **No CAPTCHA from a third party.** Altcha is self-hosted, doesn't phone home, doesn't track.

207. **Operational runbook with concrete cron snippets.** `OPERATIONS.md` ships copy-pasteable cron snippets for the operational tasks that need to be automated (weekly ACT minting, TLS certificate expiry monitoring, certbot renewal verification). Operators set these up once and the runbook walks through verifying each one. *Database backup automation is documented as paper-key + manual snapshot procedures — automated `pg_dump` cron is on the operations roadmap, not yet shipped.*

208. **QR sign-in: scan with your phone, never type your seed on a strange computer.** When you have Morphit set up on your phone, you can sign into Morphit on any computer by scanning a one-time QR — your phone shows a confirmation card with the website name (so you can spot phishing like `morph1t.io`), you tap "Yes, that was me", and a signed-and-encrypted permission slip routes through the operator (who can't decrypt it) to the computer. Posting key never leaves the phone. The crypto module ships with 29 self-checking smoke scenarios covering every validation gate (replay defense, echo checks, freshness window, signature verify, buffer-wipe defense via `sodium.memzero`) plus a 12-scenario relay-side state-machine smoke. Production phone-side signer wires the user's unlocked posting key through dblurt's secp256k1 primitive against a domain-separated signing digest (`SHA-256("morphit-pairing-v1\n" || canonical_bundle_bytes)`); the desktop verifier fetches `posting.key_auths` via the chain rotator and recovers the signing pubkey from the same digest. Domain separation prevents a captured pairing signature from ever being replayed as a chain-transaction signature, and vice versa. The phone pre-checks the user's posting authority on chain before signing — if the account uses multi-key posting (multisig) or the user's posting key isn't currently authorized, the user sees a specific actionable message ("This account uses multi-key sign-in, which this version doesn't support yet — sign in with your seed phrase instead") rather than a confusing generic failure on the desktop. Confirmation card URL displayed with explicit `dir="ltr"` so RTL-override homoglyph attacks (U+202E) can't reverse the rendered URL on RTL locales. **Honest disclosure:** multisig accounts (posting authority requiring multiple signatures) are not yet supported — single signature must clear `weight_threshold` alone. Multisig support requires extending the bundle to carry multiple signatures, which is filed as future work. See ADR-0022 for the full protocol spec, threat model, and remaining backlog.

209. **Adding a new traded asset (4th coin) is a single-package edit.** The canonical asset list (BTC, XMR, BLURT) lives in `packages/asset-registry/` — one file. Add an entry there, write an explorer-fee-verifier mirroring `bitcoinExplorerVerifier.ts` / `moneroExplorerVerifier.ts`, drop a frontend UI extension (logo, accent), add i18n strings. Pre-2026-05 the same change required edits at 32 separate sites across `apps/indexer`, `apps/relay`, `apps/web`, and `packages/indexer-client`. The package is runtime-immutable: `Object.freeze` deep on `ASSETS` plus a `Proxy` trap on `ASSET_TICKERS_SET` that throws on `add`/`delete`/`clear` — defense-in-depth against any `(x as any)` escape that could otherwise inject a fake ticker. Smoke at `packages/asset-registry/scripts/asset-registry-smoke.ts` enforces the invariants (exactly one coordination chain, no duplicate tickers, every entry has all required fields, address-shape regex accepts known-valid addresses and rejects obvious typos, runtime mutation throws).

210. **Adding a new language is a single-array edit (and the framework knows the difference between "shipped" and "in progress").** `apps/web/src/lib/i18n/index.ts` has two arrays: `SUPPORTED_LOCALES` (10 entries today: en, es, de, pl, fr, it, ru, fa, zh-CN, zh-HK) drives the language switcher; `PLANNED_LOCALES` (7 entries today: hi, ar, bn, pt, id, ja, vi) advertises work-in-progress translations without exposing them to users. The `register()` calls are loop-driven from `SUPPORTED_LOCALES` so adding a language is just adding to that array (plus dropping a JSON file). Pre-2026-05 required keeping the array and the explicit `register('xx', ...)` calls in sync manually — easy to miss one. A locale graduates from PLANNED → SUPPORTED with a one-line array move. Smoke at `apps/web/scripts/i18n-locale-registry-smoke.ts` enforces consistency (disjointness, every SUPPORTED has a JSON file, no orphan files, en is always SUPPORTED) plus a parser sanity guard so a regex miss can't silently degrade to "0 SUPPORTED locales" passing CI. Translator workflow documented in `docs/CONTRIBUTING-TRANSLATIONS.md` with quality bar (machine translation as starting point only, native-speaker review required) and per-language audience rationale.

211. **Pre-launch migration history collapsed into a single canonical schema.** `apps/indexer/src/db/schema.sql` is a single 1,834-line file that produces byte-for-byte the same Postgres state as applying the original 27 incremental migrations in order — verified via `pg_dump --schema-only` diff (zero lines diff). The 27 historical files are archived under `apps/indexer/src/db/historical/` for archaeology but never read by the runtime. The migration runner uses a `subsumesVersions: [2..27]` mechanism so a fresh DB still records all 27 versions in `schema_migrations` (preserving any downstream "is v15 applied?" check). `validateMigrationsContract()` enforces declared+subsumed coverage gap-free at module load. **Side win:** the integration test harness was unified with the production migration runner — pre-collapse, the harness had its own SQL applier that bypassed `runMigrations()`, exactly the divergence that allowed earlier audits' missing-version regressions to slip past CI. 70/70 integration tests pass (was 67; +3 collapse-regression tests).

212. **Witness fee alerts carry actionable delta information, not just before/after numbers.** `apps/indexer/src/indexer/witnessFeePoller.ts` polls Blurt's `account_creation_fee` chain parameter every hour. When the value changes, it emits a `FEE_CHANGED` alert containing `oldBlurt`, `newBlurt`, `deltaBlurt` (signed amount), `deltaPct` (signed percentage of old, `null` only on the unusual oldBlurt=0 case), and `direction` ('up' | 'down'). Operator Discord/Matrix bot integrations can pick urgency and emoji from these directly without re-doing the math. The default alert sink logs all five fields in the structured `fee_changed` warn line. Not a threshold-based alerter (BLURT account_creation_fee has been stable at 100 BLURT for years — there's no noise to filter); alerting on every change with rich context is the right behavior for this domain.

213. **IPv6 prefix bucketing applied uniformly at every relay endpoint that uses `clientIp()`.** All three rate-limited endpoints (`/v1/account/availability`, `/v1/invite`, `/v1/account/create`) wrap the client IP through `canonicalBucketKey()` before passing to the rate limiter. This collapses IPv4 to /24 and IPv6 to /64 — preventing an attacker controlling a residential /48 (65k /64s) or a small VPS /56 (256 /64s) from bypassing per-IP limits via address rotation. A permanent smoke at `apps/relay/scripts/ip-bucketing-canonicalization-smoke.ts` greps every file under `apps/relay/src/api/` and fails CI if any endpoint calls `clientIp()` without also calling `canonicalBucketKey()`, so a future endpoint added without the canonicalization wrap surfaces in CI rather than shipping silently bypassable. 13 unit tests in `apps/relay/test/middleware/ip.test.ts` cover the canonicalization edge cases (zone identifiers, double-`::`, empty hextets, mixed casing, IPv4-mapped IPv6, deprecated forms).

214. **CSP and security headers consistently emitted at every nginx location.** All four nginx surfaces (`ops/nginx/web.conf`, `relay.conf`, `indexer.conf`, plus the per-app middleware in `apps/relay/src/middleware/security.ts` and `apps/indexer/src/api/middleware/security.ts`) ship strict Content-Security-Policy headers. The frontend's web.conf survived a real defense-in-depth gap during the May 2026 audit: the per-`location` `add_header` directives (for caching policy on `/_app/immutable/`, `/service-worker.js`, and the SPA fallback) were silently dropping the server-block-level security-header inheritance — the nginx footgun where any `location`-scoped `add_header` zeroes inheritance. Bundle JS, the service worker, and HTML responses had been going out without HSTS / CSP / X-Frame-Options / Referrer-Policy / X-Content-Type-Options. Now every location block re-emits the full security-header set explicitly, with the footgun comment so future edits don't regress.

227. **Active/Owner key handling audit — black-hat-mindset deep dive (2026-05-07).** Every code path that touches the user's `active` or `owner` private key was hand-traced from the user-input layer through the keystore decrypt, the JIT-unlock pattern, the dblurt PrivateKey wrapper, the secp256k1 ecdsaSign call, and back to the wipe in the `finally` block. Findings: (1) `LiveIdentity` structurally cannot hold active/owner privates — TypeScript types only expose `ownerPublicKey: Uint8Array \| null` and `activePublicKey: Uint8Array \| null`, no private-key fields, so a "just grab it from live identity" code path is impossible to write. (2) `useJitKey` (the shared internal of `useActiveKey`/`useOwnerKey`) wipes every other role's private key plus seedBytes BEFORE handing the requested role to the callback, then wipes that role's key in `finally` regardless of success or throw — the JS engine cannot keep the key alive past the callback frame. (3) The M6 cross-tab envelope-replacement defense is a constant-time pubkey-pin check inside `useJitKey`: if a hostile same-origin tab plants an envelope decrypting to a different identity under the same password, useJitKey wipes the attacker's keys and throws `identity_mismatch` rather than handing those keys to the broadcast callback. (4) dblurt's `PrivateKey` constructor stores its 32-byte input by reference (verified at `node_modules/@beblurt/dblurt/lib/crypto.js` line 273) — when `useJitKey` memzeros the buffer, the dblurt wrapper's internal reference now points to zeroed bytes, harmless after the wipe. (5) Every active-key call site (`FeatureBidForm`, `PayBlurtModal`, `StrangerFeeModal`, post route) clears the user's password on both success and error paths. The HardwareKeyCard enroll/soften flows were tightened in this audit to clear passwords on the error path too (previously only on success/cancel). The PayBlurtModal early-exit (when `prepareUnsignedTransfer` throws before reaching `runWithActiveKey`) was tightened to clear the password before returning. The login route, onboarding new-account flow, onboarding/import seed-keyfile path, onboarding/import posting-only path, and onboarding's leave-confirmation handler were all tightened to clear their password component-state before navigating away on success — explicit clears shorten the heap-residency window beyond the GC-only cleanup that component unmount provides. (6) Sourcemaps disabled in production builds (`apps/web/vite.config.js` line 25) so `activePriv` and `wanted` get minified, raising the cost of targeted heap-scraping. (7) Zero analytics, zero error-reporters, zero telemetry — error stacks never leave the device. (8) Honest residual: the encrypt/decrypt layer goes through `JSON.stringify`/`JSON.parse` with base64-encoded private keys; the intermediate JS strings are immutable and cannot be `sodium.memzero`'d, living on the heap until GC. Same fundamental K1.2 constraint that affected the mnemonic before we switched to seedBytes. Cannot be eliminated without dropping JSON entirely. Documented inline in `keystore.ts` and in `SECURITY.md` §1b so future maintainers don't think it's solved by `sodium.memzero(plaintext)` alone. Audit findings are committed in `SECURITY.md` §1b "Active/Owner key deep-audit findings (2026-05-07)" with the full reasoning trail. 234. **`drain-defense-live-fire` cross-session flake root-caused (encoding-padding ambiguity in smoke's fault-injection) and fixed (2026-05-08, Part 85).** A long-standing intermittent flake — blamed across multiple sessions on "tsx-warmup timing race" — finally root-caused as a defect in the smoke's fault-injection logic, not a timing issue at all. The `'tampered signature rejected'` scenario flipped the LAST char of the base64url-encoded HMAC-SHA-256 signature to test that `InviteTokenService.verify` rejects tampered sigs. The math: HMAC-SHA-256 → 32 bytes → 256 bits, encoded as 43 base64url chars carrying 258 bits. **The last 2 bits of the trailing char are encoding padding** — they decode to nothing, and 4 distinct last-chars share the same 4-bit prefix and decode to the identical 32-byte buffer. For a random sig, the chance the tamper char ('a' or 'b' depending on the original) lands in the "padding-equivalent" set is ~6%. Empirically: **59 collisions in 1000 trials.** When a collision hit, the "tampered" sig decoded to the SAME bytes as the original; `verify()` correctly accepted it; the scenario's assertion fired. The flake appeared cold-tsx-correlated because triple-pulse on hot caches usually all-passed and cold cycles between sessions were more likely to expose the probabilistic failure across more total trials — but cold tsx doesn't increase the collision probability, only the variance in how many trials catch it. **Fix (one line at apps/relay/scripts/drain-defense-live-fire.ts:230):** tamper the FIRST char instead. The first 6 bits of base64 always encode the high 6 bits of byte 0; any change is guaranteed to change the first decoded byte. **Empirical post-fix: 0 collisions in 10,000 random-sig trials; 20+ consecutive cold-tsx cycles clean.** Pre-fix flake rate ~6%; post-fix observed 0 across 20 cold-cycle runs (statistical confidence ≥ 70% by observation; mathematical confidence near-100% by encoding analysis). **Production code was always correct** — HMAC verify, timingSafeEqual, base64 decode all behave as intended. The bug was entirely in the test harness. **Lessons documented in REVISIT-LIST and memory:** fault-injection in tests must produce GUARANTEED semantic differences, not textual ones (textual flips can be neutralized by encoding-layer ambiguity); a fault-injection assertion that fails ~6% is a smell of probabilistic distribution over a discrete encoding space (~1/16 ≈ 4 bits of insufficient discrimination, which is exactly what base64's 6-bit-char vs 8-bit-byte ratio leaks at the trailing edge of a 32-byte payload). 256. **No Morphit instance — not even canonical morphit.io — holds any verification secret for Monero fees. Every instance verifies every XMR payment independently using user-generated per-payment proofs (Part 108++).** This is the strongest privacy + decentralization posture we know how to build. **The privacy story:** when you pay a Monero fee on Morphit, your wallet generates a tiny one-time proof string (`OutProofV2…`) that confirms — to anyone who cares to check — that this specific transaction sent this specific amount to Morphit's treasury address.
